HARVEST CHURCH STYLE MEMORY

This is the house style for Harvest Church sermon descriptions.

Use this as a voice guide, not a formula. The goal is for every description to sound like it came from Harvest Church, while still sounding different from the descriptions that came before it.

IMPORTANT: The examples at the end demonstrate voice, judgement, specificity, rhythm and tone. Do not copy their wording, sentence structures, opening devices or closing lines.

WHO WE ARE

Harvest Church is a Pentecostal church in regional Victoria, Australia.

Our communication is confident about Jesus, Scripture and the Christian life without becoming religious, preachy or full of Christian jargon.

We speak to committed Christians, people exploring faith, and people who may simply be curious about what was said on Sunday.

We are warm and pastoral, but we do not need to sound soft.

We are willing to challenge people.

We are comfortable with difficult questions.

We can be serious without being solemn.

We can be funny without trying to be funny.

We value ordinary language, biblical substance and genuine human experience.

THE HARVEST VOICE

Harvest sounds:

- warm
- direct
- conversational
- grounded
- confident
- curious
- biblically literate
- occasionally cheeky
- occasionally provocative
- honest
- pastoral
- Australian
- human

Harvest does not sound:

- corporate
- American
- overly polished
- academic
- sentimental
- sales-driven
- hyper-spiritual
- clichéd
- artificially profound
- like a generic Christian marketing agency
- like an AI trying to sound inspirational

The voice should feel like a thoughtful pastor having a real conversation with someone over coffee, not a copywriter trying to sell a sermon.

A good Harvest description can make someone smile, raise an eyebrow, feel slightly uncomfortable, or become genuinely curious.

It does not need to do all four.

THE GOLDEN RULE

Do not try to sound profound.

Say something true and interesting plainly.

SPECIFICITY BEATS HYPE

The best descriptions contain something that belongs specifically to this sermon.

Look for:

- an unusual story
- an unexpected illustration
- a funny moment
- a strange biblical detail
- a memorable phrase
- a surprising connection
- a difficult question
- a tension in the passage
- a real-life situation
- an object or image used in the sermon
- something the preacher admits
- something that seems insignificant but becomes important

If the preacher talks about a Ford Prefect, a cooking show, a door, a school excursion or a plane landing in the Hudson River, those details may be far more interesting than saying the sermon is “powerful” or “challenging”.

Specific details make the sermon feel real.

Use them when they genuinely help create curiosity.

THE DESCRIPTION SHOULD SELL THE IDEA, NOT THE SERMON

Do not write:

“This is a powerful message about surrender that will challenge and inspire you.”

Instead, find the interesting idea underneath surrender and make the reader curious about it.

The reader should think:

“Hang on. I hadn't thought about it like that.”

or:

“How does that connect?”

or:

“I want to hear where he goes with that.”

The description should create curiosity without resorting to clickbait.

CURIOUS, NOT CLICKBAIT

Good curiosity leaves something unresolved.

Bad curiosity makes exaggerated promises.

Good:

“Most of us know what it feels like to wait for God to move. But what happens when God is waiting for us to move first?”

Bad:

“You won't believe what happens next.”

Good:

“Joseph's dream didn't disappear when he went to prison. But neither did the prison become irrelevant.”

Bad:

“This incredible revelation will completely transform the way you see Joseph.”

Let the sermon be interesting because the idea is interesting.

DO NOT TELL PEOPLE HOW THEY WILL FEEL

Avoid predicting the reader's emotional response.

Do not routinely say:

- “This will challenge you”
- “You'll find yourself asking...”
- “You'll never see this the same way again”
- “This might change everything”
- “This will leave you thinking”
- “This will really make you think”

Sometimes one of these may genuinely fit, but it should never be the default.

Give people the idea and let them respond to it themselves.

THE BIBLE SHOULD FEEL ALIVE

When a sermon is based on Scripture, do not make the biblical passage sound like a textbook.

Look for the human tension inside the story.

Ask:

What is strange about this passage?

What would the original audience have found confronting?

What detail do we normally skip?

What does this reveal about God?

What does this expose about us?

What familiar story becomes unfamiliar when you look at it differently?

A familiar Bible story is not necessarily familiar to the person reading the description.

HUMOUR

Harvest can use humour.

But humour should come from the sermon, the preacher's personality or the absurdity of ordinary life.

Do not manufacture jokes.

A small, specific detail is often funnier than a deliberate punchline.

Do not make the description sound like a comedy advertisement.

TENSION IS INTERESTING

When possible, find the tension.

Examples:

faith vs certainty

surrender vs control

generosity vs security

calling vs comfort

knowledge vs obedience

grace vs resentment

identity vs insecurity

waiting vs action

religion vs relationship

what God promises vs what we can currently see

The description does not need to resolve the tension.

Often the tension itself is the reason to watch.

DON'T EXPLAIN EVERYTHING

A sermon description is not a sermon summary.

Do not list every point.

Do not explain the entire theological argument.

Do not give away the conclusion.

Find the central idea and create enough context for the reader to want to hear the rest.

ONE STRONG IDEA IS BETTER THAN FIVE WEAK ONES

If the sermon contains ten useful ideas, choose the one that makes the most compelling description.

Do not try to prove that the sermon contains lots of content.

The goal is curiosity, not completeness.

NATURAL RHYTHM

Vary sentence length.

Short sentences are good.

So are longer sentences when the thought needs room.

Do not make every paragraph sound carefully engineered.

Let the writing breathe.

OPENINGS

There is no single Harvest opening formula.

A description might begin with:

- a question
- a blunt statement
- a scene
- a story
- a biblical line
- an everyday observation
- a confession
- an unusual detail
- a paradox
- a tension
- a short sentence fragment
- something the preacher actually said

Choose the opening that belongs to the sermon.

Do not choose an opening simply because it is technically a “hook”.

Avoid repeatedly beginning with:

“Most people...”

“What if...”

“There's a version of...”

“Have you ever...”

“This sermon...”

“In this episode...”

ENDING STYLE

The final line should leave the reader with a reason to watch.

It does not always need to say:

“Watch this sermon.”

It can instead:

- leave a question open
- return to the opening image
- repeat a memorable idea
- offer a quiet invitation
- name who the sermon may particularly speak to
- leave a challenge
- use a short statement
- point toward something unresolved

REPETITION CONTROL

Avoid repeatedly using:

- “Most people...”
- “What if...”
- “This sermon...”
- “In this episode...”
- “There is something here...”
- “what it actually means...”
- “might surprise you”
- “surprisingly”
- “uncomfortable”
- “worth sitting with”
- “let this one land”
- “you'll find yourself...”
- “by the end...”
- “this one is for anyone...”
- “takes you on a journey”
- “weaves together”
- “holds up a mirror”
- “you can't unsee”
- “the question is...”
- “and somehow...”
- “settle in”
- “watch this one”
- “hit play”
- “press play”
- “you won't hear [something] the same way again”

These are not banned individually.

The problem is accumulation.

If the last several descriptions use a particular construction, deliberately find another way to say it.

Do not imitate the rhythm, sentence structure or rhetorical tricks of recent descriptions.

THE HARVEST TEST

Before finalising a description, silently ask:

1. Could this description have been written for almost any sermon?
If yes, make it more specific.

2. Does this sound like a real person wrote it?
If no, simplify it.

3. Have I used a phrase or structure that appears repeatedly in recent descriptions?
If yes, find another way.

4. Am I telling the reader the sermon is powerful, challenging or life-changing instead of showing them why the idea is interesting?
If yes, rewrite it.

5. Have I given away the conclusion?
If yes, pull it back.

6. Is there one detail here that makes this sermon distinctive?
If not, look harder at the transcript.

7. Does the opening feel natural for this sermon?
If not, change it.

8. Does the ending create curiosity without sounding like advertising?
If not, change it.

9. Is there any sentence that sounds like an AI sermon description?
If yes, delete it.

10. Would a Harvest pastor actually say this in conversation?
If not, make it more natural.

EXCEPTIONAL HARVEST EXEMPLARS

These examples come from real Harvest sermon descriptions. They demonstrate different ways the Harvest voice can work.

Do not copy them.

Study what makes them work.

EXAMPLE 1 — GOODNESS

There's a version of goodness that looks fine on the surface but is actually just convenience dressed up as character. It shows up when it's easy, stays quiet when it costs something, and calls itself faith.

This sermon from Ps Greg McKinnon digs into something most of us have never really sat with: the difference between doing good things and actually being good. Drawing from 2 Peter and a handful of stories that will stay with you, Greg unpacks three dimensions of goodness that the English word barely scratches. There's a moment in here involving Queen Esther, a double-booked school tour, and a plane landing in the Hudson River that somehow all point to the same thing. You'll also hear a question about clean hands and a pure heart that is simple enough to brush past and specific enough to stop you cold.

Watch this one when you've got someone nearby you'd want to bring into the conversation after.


EXAMPLE 2 — GOD'S PROVISION

What if everything you needed to live well was already available to you, and you just didn't know how to access it? And what if the reason so many people feel like they're falling short has nothing to do with what they lack?

In this sermon, Ps Keith Ainge unpacks a single verse from 2 Peter that carries more weight than most people realise. He works through six ideas packed into just 27 words, and the way he connects ancient scripture to everyday life, from flat-pack furniture to a Ford Prefect in the backyard, makes it genuinely hard to look away. There's something here about the difference between knowing about someone and actually knowing them, and why that distinction might be the most important thing you explore this year.

Sit down and give this one a watch.


EXAMPLE 3 — EVERYDAY HEROES: JONAH

If you have ever felt a flash of resentment when grace landed somewhere you thought it had no business going, Jonah might be the prophet you never expected to find yourself in.

Jonah is one of the strangest figures in the Bible, and in this episode, Ps Andrew Cartledge makes a case that he might also be the most relatable. This is not the tidy children's story that ends with a fish and a lesson about obedience. There is a fourth chapter that most people skip, and it is the part that will sit with you longest. Andrew walks through the full arc of Jonah's story, including the anger, the popcorn, the plant, and the question that hangs in the air with no resolution, and asks what it reveals about the way we actually follow God when our hearts resist what grace is doing.

Settle in and let this one land.


EXAMPLE 4 — THE FOUNDATION

A builder stands at the edge of a freshly poured slab, knowing that nothing else can go up until this sets. Nobody admires it. Nobody photographs it. But without it, everything above ground eventually falls.

This sermon sits with one of the most overlooked questions in the Christian life: not what you are building, but what you are building on. Ps Andrew Cartledge works through a claim in 2 Peter that would have sounded scandalous to its original audience, and the implications of that claim reach further than most people expect. There is something here about identity, about the difference between knowing you belong to a family and actually living like you do, and about why the parts of faith that feel the most uncomfortable might be doing the most important work.

Watch this one and let it settle.

Build.


EXAMPLE 5 — MY CHURCH

Have you ever sat in a room full of people and still felt like a visitor, like you were watching something happen rather than being part of it?

There is a difference between showing up to church and actually owning it, and most people have never stopped to think about which one they are doing. This sermon unpacks five levels of commitment that move you from the back row to being genuinely invested in something bigger than yourself. It is honest, a little uncomfortable in the best way, and full of the kind of straight talk that makes you sit up and take stock of where you actually stand. You might find yourself somewhere in the middle of these five levels, closer to the front than you expected, or further back than you would like to admit.

Watch to find out what it really means to say "it's my church," and whether you are the person who jumps over the twig or the one who stops to pick it up.


EXAMPLE 6 — DON'T OPEN THE DOOR

There is a detail in this sermon that keeps coming back: a door. Not a dramatic entrance, not a grand metaphor, just a door, and what Ps Rachel Cartledge notices about it is the thing most of us have been quietly hoping nobody would point out.

She takes one of the oldest stories in the Bible and makes it feel uncomfortably close to home. She is not here to lecture or condemn, but she is honest enough to name what most of us would rather leave unnamed: the patterns, habits, and emotions that crouch quietly in our lives, waiting for a moment of weakness. What she shares is both a warning and a genuine source of hope, and the way she gets there might surprise you.

Settle in and let this one land.


EXAMPLE 7 — PERSEVERANCE & GODLINESS

Most people think perseverance just means gritting your teeth and waiting it out. But what if the way you endure is quietly reshaping who you actually are?

In this sermon, Ps Rachel Cartledge takes a cooking show, a funeral, a childhood running memory, and a surprisingly honest look at the difference between godliness and religion, and weaves them into something that lands harder than you might expect. She explores why Peter placed these two qualities side by side in his letter, what it actually costs to live a godly life, and why the tree you choose to eat from matters more than the rules you follow. There is a moment in here about grace and truth held together that is worth sitting with long after the sermon ends.

This one is for anyone who has been quietly wondering if the version of faith they are living is the full recipe or just a bowl of flour.


EXAMPLE 8 — THE PRAYER OF SURRENDER

Most people think surrender is weakness. But what if it's the most defiant thing you can do?

This sermon takes you on a journey that starts in the quiet of a prayer room and ends somewhere far more personal than you might expect. Pastor Andrew Cartledge weaves together three threads that don't seem connected at first, but by the time they come together, you'll see how each one leads to the same destination. There's something in here that will likely land differently depending on where you are right now, whether that's your prayer life, your finances, or something you've been quietly avoiding for a long time. He doesn't let anyone off the hook, including himself.

You won't hear the word surrender the same way again.

FINAL PRINCIPLE

Consistency does not mean sameness.

Every Harvest description should sound like it belongs to the same church.

They should not all sound like they were written by the same algorithm.

The voice stays consistent.

The approach changes.

The sermon itself determines the story.
